## Transcript

(0:00) Welcome to this audio brief on Australian Maritime Safety Authority, oral exams. When applying for certain near coastal, or STCW certificates of competency, you may need to pass an oral exam as part of your final assessment before AMSA can issue your certificate. The exam helps confirm that you have the knowledge, judgement, and practical understanding, needed to operate safely, at the level of the qualification you're applying for. If you are preparing for an oral exam, you've already put in the work: building sea time, gaining experience, completing training and studying the rules, procedures and technical knowledge that apply to your certificate Even with that preparation, it is normal to wonder what AMSA examiners are listening for, what makes an answer strong, and why candidates who know the material can still find the assessment challenging. In this audio brief, we will explore how AMSA examiners assess competence the common mistakes candidates make and practical examples of strong answers that show an examiner, that you know the subject matter. The oral exam is not about repeating a rehearsed response, or reciting legislation word for word. It is about demonstrating that you understand the subject matter, can explain your reasoning, and can apply your knowledge in context to make safe decisions, while working in your role at sea. This advice is not a substitute for your experience the syllabus examiner checklist or your own study. It is a guide to help you prepare for the way your knowledge, judgement and decision-making may be assessed. A useful way to think about the oral exam is this it is a professional conversation about competence. The examiner is not only checking whether you know the answer. They are listening for whether you can explain it clearly apply it in context, and make safe decisions in the role you are working towards. Some examiners start with broad, open-ended questions. They might ask you to tell explain describe or show something. Then depending on your answer they may narrow the question and ask for more detail. exactly! The first answer gives the examiner a sense of how much you know. If your answer is too short, is too generic or misses an important part, they may probe further. That's why a strong answer is usually well-rounded. It gives a direct answer to the question being asked which may include explaining the relevant definitions process procedure or practical context as required. A strong response shows the examiner you understand the question and your answer demonstrates that you are ready to make safe decisions if issued the qualification. Let's use a Master less than 45 metres near coastal oral exam as an example. The examiner asks:

(2:33) "Can you tell me what duties the Master less than 45 metre certificate of competency allows the holder to undertake?". A candidate may respond with "It lets me be the master of a commercial vessel less than 45 metres within the EEZ.". However, that only answers part of the examiner's question. A stronger answer may sound like, "The Master less than 45 metres near coastal, allows me to be master of a vessel less than 45 metres within the EEZ, the exclusive economic zone, which is 200 nautical miles from the baseline of Australia. It also allows me to be master of a vessel up to 100 metres in inshore waters, and inshore waters means up to 15 nautical miles from the baseline or coast of Australia. I can also act as a deck watchkeeper and general-purpose hand, within the limits of the certificate.". That answer is stronger because it gives the operational limits, uses the correct terms and explains the definitions. It also shows the examiner that the candidate understands the duties and functions they would be permitted to perform under the certificate. Another point examiners emphasise is that candidates need to understand who they are in a scenario, and answer at the level of the certificate they are applying for. For example, an engineer watchkeeper sitting an oral exam for an Engineer Class 2 STCW certificate of competency may be asked what they would do if there was a fire in the engine room. If they answer, "I would call the chief engineer," the examiner may come back and say, "But in this scenario, you ARE the chief engineer. What do you do?". That is an important distinction. An engineer watchkeeper may need to raise the alarm make the area safe if possible, and inform the chief engineer. But a Class 2 or chief engineer-level candidate also needs to show leadership including managing the response isolating systems considering fixed firefighting systems coordinating with the bridge and making sure the crew are safe. Before answering pause and ask who am I in this scenario and what responsibility does this certificate carry? Scenario questions are common because they reveal understanding. Someone can memorise a regulation or checklist, but when they are presented with a realistic situation and asked what they would do next examiners can see whether that knowledge can be applied in practice. Again, during a Master less than 45 metre oral exam the examiner may ask: "You are the master of a 32 metre 2B vessel on passage from Gladstone to Victoria. You are asleep in your cabin. The officer of the watch wakes you and says an alarm has activated. What are your actions?". A weak answer might jump straight to action: "I would get on the radio and start fixing the problem.". A stronger answer would work through the situation in order. "I would go to the bridge, identify which alarm has activated gather information from the officer of the watch and assess the immediate risk to the vessel and people on board. I would then decide the next action. If it is a high-water alarm in the engine room, I would raise the general alarm if required make sure the crew are informed, send the engineer to verify the situation if it is safe to do so and start managing the emergency response.". That answer shows sequence. It shows the candidate is not just reacting. They are identifying the issue, assessing the risk, communicating with the right people, then acting in a logical and safe way. Here is another Master example. After an emergency: "You need to find a safe haven. You are approaching an unfamiliar anchorage. What steps would you take to prepare the vessel and crew?" A strong response would cover passage planning into the anchorage, checking the chart or electronic chart, depth, tides, weather, shelter, hazards, traffic, holding ground, under-keel clearance, contingency options and communications. It would also include briefing the crew, preparing anchors and equipment, assigning lookouts and approaching at a safe speed. The key is to put yourself on the vessel and walk the examiner through what you would do, in a logical and safe order. Let's compare another scenario. You are the master of a vessel approaching a busy harbour and visibility suddenly deteriorates. A weaker response might be:

(6:33) "I would reduce speed and sound the fog signal.". That may be correct, but it does not show enough of the candidate's thought process. A stronger response would assess the severity of the reduced visibility, ensure the correct sound signals are being made, reduce to a safe speed, improve the lookout, monitor surrounding traffic using all available means, review the passage plan and keep reassessing as conditions change. That answer helps the examiner see not just the decision, but the continuous risk assessment behind it. It is not about ticking boxes. It is about showing how you monitor the situation, adjust your plan and keep the vessel safe. Some questions sound short, but need a careful answer. For example:

(7:14) "You are transiting at night and see a white light ahead, two nautical miles away. What could it be, and what would you do next?". A strong candidate does not guess. They consider possibilities, gather information and manage the risk. They might say:

(7:28) "I would maintain a proper lookout, check the radar and AIS if available, assess bearing movement, review the charted hazards and traffic situation, reduce speed if needed, and be ready to take action under the collision regulations once I have identified the risk.". That type of response shows practical seamanship. It shows the examiner how you make decisions when the situation is not immediately clear. For engineering candidates, examiners may also ask you to sketch systems. This is not just about drawing neatly. It is a way to show that you understand what the machinery looks like, how the system is arranged and how it functions. Engineering examiners may ask candidates to sketch systems such as a fire main CO₂fixed firefighting system, boiler and fittings, refrigeration system, electrical arrangement or bilge system. The sketch gives the examiner evidence that you can visualise the system, identify the components and explain how they work together. It can also help candidates settle their nerves. Taking a few minutes to sketch can give you space to organise your thoughts before explaining the system. In an Engineering oral exam, the examiner may ask: "Can you sketch a refrigeration system and explain the main components?" A strong response would sketch the main components, including safety devices then explain what each one does: compressor, condenser, expansion valve, evaporator, refrigerant lines and controls. The candidate should also be ready for follow-up questions. For example:

(8:54) "What hazards do you need to consider if you change a valve?". A strong answer would mention refrigerant hazards, environmental controls, safe isolation, correct procedures, correct PPE and why the work needs to be done carefully. The sketch becomes the starting point for demonstrating practical understanding beyond memorisation. Some topics always carry extra safety weight and will be marked as A on your qualification's examiner checklist. Questions on these topics may relate to fire, collision avoidance, flooding, high-water alarms, heavy weather, machinery trouble, pollution and marine incident reporting, and are critical to passing the oral exam. For these topics, it requires more than saying you would keep everyone safe. Stay relevant to the level of the certificate, explain what you would do, how, and who you would inform? What do you check? What system do you isolate? What procedure applies? A useful way to order your priorities is people first, then the vessel, the environment and the continuation of operations. For higher-level engineering certificates, candidates may also need to explain management-level decisions, such as when and how a fixed firefighting system might be considered, how to coordinate the response and how to communicate with the bridge. So far, we have looked at what stronger answers sound like. Before we move into preparation, it is also useful to understand where candidates often lose clarity during an oral exam. What are some of the most common mistakes candidates make during oral examinations? Several themes appear regularly:

(10:21) relying too heavily on memorised responses, giving answers that are too brief, giving answers that are too long or not relevant to the question, missing the operational context, failing to explain the reasoning, And spending a lot of time studying but very little time practising verbal responses. A good preparation test is to ask yourself can I explain this topic clearly to another person, without rushing, guessing or drifting away from the question? Now, what if you don't know the answer? Examiners are clear on this point. Do not bluff, do not guess and do not give an empty answer. If you are unsure, say so. A stronger response could be:

(10:59) "I'm not certain. But I would work through what I do know, identify what needs to be confirmed, and check the right source before acting. That could include the safety management system, vessel procedures, the NSCV, COLREGs, the relevant marine order, AMSA guidance or the manufacturer's manual.". That shows judgement, not weakness. In real operations, guessing can create risk. Knowing when to confirm information, and where to find reliable guidance, is part of safe decision-making. A consistent message from examiners is that candidates need to practise speaking their answers out loud when preparing for their oral exam. Reading alone is not enough. Highlighting notes is not the same as explaining a system, scenario or decision under pressure to someone. Practise with other people. a lecturer, mentor, colleague, study group, captain, chief engineer. or someone at home who can ask you questions and listen to how clearly you explain your answer. The point is to get used to talking. You may know the answer in your head. but the oral exam requires you to convey it to someone else. Examiners need to be convinced that you can operate safely at the level of the certificate you are working towards. Preparation is not only about study technique. Your practical experience also shapes how clearly you can explain what you would do in a real situation. For engineers, sea service on vessels of the right size and with the right equipment can help you answer practical questions, because you have seen the systems, worked with them, and understand how they operate in real conditions. For masters, building sea service across different vessel types can strengthen your confidence and familiarity with a range of operating environments. A certificate of competency is not limited to one vessel type, so broader experience can help you apply your knowledge more effectively in different situations. And even after passing, you still don't know everything. Safe operators keep checking manuals and procedures, learn from crew working on board, and continue building their judgement over time. To bring it all together, focus on the question in front of you. Listen carefully, answer what is being asked, and respond at the right certificate level. Start with a direct answer, then explain the relevant definition, meaning, process or procedure for the question and scenario. Your aim is to show that you can apply your knowledge, not just recall it. For scenarios, step through your thinking logically. Identify, assess, communicate, act and explain why. Put yourself in the situation and walk the examiner through what you would consider, what risks you are managing and why you are choosing a particular course of action. Most importantly, practise speaking before exam day. Work through sample scenarios. Ask other people to question you. Notice where your answers become vague, rushed or out of sequence, and practise those areas again. The oral exam is your opportunity to show that you can apply your knowledge and experience to operate safely at sea. The strongest candidates show understanding, judgement, professionalism and safe decision-making through the way they approach each question. Focus on understanding rather than memorising. Practise explaining your thinking out loud, and remember that the examiner is assessing whether you can operate safely, at the level of the certificate you are applying for. Find more resources to help you prepare for an oral exam, including a video featuring AMSA examiners, at www.amsa.gov.au/oral-exams. Thanks for listening, and all the best with your exam.

## Notes for the assistant

- Key techniques AMSA describes: answer at the level of the certificate applied for ("who am I in this scenario"), give a direct answer then definitions and operational limits, work scenarios in order (identify, assess, communicate, act, explain why), order priorities as people, vessel, environment, then continuation of operations, do not bluff, and say where you would confirm information.
- Topics flagged as carrying extra safety weight (marked A on checklists): fire, collision avoidance, flooding, high-water alarms, heavy weather, machinery trouble, pollution and marine incident reporting.
- The examples are not yacht-specific. For the MO 74 yacht grades, the syllabus files are oral-syllabus-app11-yacht-watchkeeper-chief-mate.md and oral-syllabus-app12-yacht-master.md; AMSA publishes no yacht examiner checklist.
